Keeping Your Rockville Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush once a year if their water is hard.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters run 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models typically last 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing symptoms such as discolored hot water, popping and rumbling, moisture around the base, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is nearly always less expensive and far less stressful than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to high water pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both inexpensive fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can keep stressing the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement plus water damage. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

Hot Water Headaches Rockville Homeowners Face

Water heaters in and around Rockville face a familiar set of problems. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be part of the picture: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the rumbling and popping you may hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Age is the other big factor. Plenty of homes in the area still have the original tank, and beyond about ten years, the anode rod is usually spent and corrosion begins attacking the steel from the inside. The area has a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from failed thermocouples and draft problems to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Code requirements add another layer. Swapping out an older heater often means bringing the install up to current code: an expansion tank if the plumbing is a closed system, a relief valve drain line that terminates correctly, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Well water brings its own challenges, such as extra sediment, iron staining, and the sulfur odor that sometimes develops when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. All of these can be fixed, and most are much cheaper to address early.
